FeFET Pulse Study — Updated Conclusions

Three-terminal synchronized PMU program/delay/read measurements

Updated central conclusion. The clearest FeFET-like result was obtained at VG,read = +0.5 V and VD,read = −2 V under repeated programming. The device shows reproducible, write-polarity-dependent conductance training that survives a 5 s write-to-read delay and reverses direction when the write polarity is reversed. The condition VG,read = −0.5 V and VD,read = +2 V produced the largest single-pulse retention window, but it should not be described as the overall best FeFET switching condition. Read disturbance and charge trapping are clearly present, but the matched 5 s read-only control produces only a small, mostly unidirectional drift and cannot explain the much larger reversible switching window.

1. Measurement architecture and pulse design

The FeFET was measured as a three-terminal device using two synchronized channels of one 4225-PMU/RPM system:

  • PMU CH1: gate programming and gate read bias.
  • PMU CH2: drain read pulse and measurement of IDS.
  • Source: connected to GNDU.

The generic sequence was:

gate write pulse/train → write-to-read delay t → apply VG,read and VD,read → measure CH2 IDS

Positive and negative write pulses were independently configurable in amplitude, width, rise/fall time, idle time, pulse count, and program/read repetition count. Both PMU channels used identical segment timing. Delays up to 1 s were included in one synchronized Segment-Arb execution; longer delays used separate write and read executions with a software-controlled waiting interval.

3. Single-pulse and multiple-pulse responses are different

A single write pulse often produced a small or ambiguous window. In contrast, repeated programming produced gradual and reproducible state training. This supports the hypothesis that one short pulse may not switch a sufficient fraction of ferroelectric domains to generate a large read window. However, progressive trapping can also accumulate with pulse number, so pulse-count dependence alone is not proof of ferroelectric switching.

3.1 Why a single pulse may miss the gradual FeFET-like response

The device appears to behave as a progressively switched system rather than an ideal binary element. One short write pulse may reverse only a small fraction of domains, particularly when the ferroelectric layer contains a distribution of coercive fields or when the applied pulse is close to the effective switching threshold. The resulting threshold-voltage shift after one pulse may be too small to produce an obvious drain-current window at a given read bias.

With increasing pulse number, additional regions can switch and the conductance approaches a high- or low-state plateau:

ΔVT(1 pulse) < ΔVT(N pulses),   and   IDS(N) → Ihigh/low

Charge trapping can also evolve cumulatively with pulse number. Therefore, the gradual response is consistent with progressive multidomain switching but must be interpreted together with the polarity reversibility and read-only controls.

4. Reversible bipolar training at VG,read = 0.5 V and VD,read = −2 V

This condition produced the best overall FeFET-like result in the present study. Unlike the single-pulse ranking, it revealed the formation, saturation, and reversal of two conductance states over multiple positive/negative cycles.

Repeated positive and negative write blocks produced opposite conductance evolution over several complete cycles:

  • Positive write pulses progressively increased |IDS|, driving the device toward a high-conductance state.
  • Negative write pulses progressively decreased |IDS|, driving the device toward a low-conductance state.
  • Reversing the write polarity reversed the training direction.
  • The behavior was reproduced over multiple positive/negative cycles.

Because VD,read is negative, the measured current is negative. A curve moving downward corresponds to increasing |IDS| and increasing conductance; a curve moving upward corresponds to decreasing |IDS|. Future plots should therefore use |IDS| or |IDS/VDS|.

5. Read-only controls and updated interpretation

5.1 Fast read-only control at a different bias

A fast read-only sequence at VG,read = −0.5 V, VD,read = +2 V, and 1 ms delay showed a large initial current reduction before approaching a noisy plateau. This demonstrated that repeated reading can disturb the device or expose initial settling.

5.2 Matched 5 s read-only control

The more relevant control used the same read condition as the bipolar training experiment:

VG,read = +0.5 V,   VD,read = −2 V,   delay = 5 s

Without write pulses, the current remained near approximately −1.13 to −1.16 × 10−7 A, with a slow drift of only a few nA and substantial point-to-point noise. This is much smaller than the tens-of-nA reversible excursions observed when positive and negative write pulses were applied.

Updated inference: read disturbance and trapping are present, but read disturbance alone does not reproduce the large, polarity-reversible training. The write pulses provide the dominant direction-selective contribution, while read disturbance and trapping modify the baseline, training rate, and scatter.

6. Interpretation of the mixed mechanism

The measured current can be represented phenomenologically as:

IDS(N,t) = Ipolarization(N,t) + Itrap(N,t) + Iread disturb(N,t) + Isettling(t)

Evidence supporting a polarization-related contribution includes the repeatable reversal of the training direction when the write polarity is reversed and the persistence of two distinguishable states after a 5 s delay. Evidence supporting trapping includes gradual rather than instantaneous state formation, delay-dependent window reduction, read-only drift, and possible baseline evolution during long experiments.

The present data therefore support a mixed mechanism rather than an ideal binary ferroelectric switch or a purely trapping-based memory.

7. Stress, measurement range, and possible breakdown

Repeated gate pulses near ±4 to ±5 V, drain read biases near ±2 V, and hundreds of total pulses can stress or damage the device. Some currents reached approximately 160–170 nA. CH2 should use a fixed range of at least 1 µA for these conditions. Gate leakage, measured drain voltage, PMU status, and irreversible baseline changes should be monitored. Suspected breakdown or degradation should be checked on a fresh device before drawing mechanism-level conclusions.

8. Recommended discriminating experiments

  1. Matched read-only controls: repeat the same number and timing of reads at each candidate read bias without write pulses.
  2. Interleaved polarity: alternate +write/read and −write/read to remove the long time offset between positive and negative blocks.
  3. Preset-controlled pulse-number sweep: use N = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, and 30, with the same preset state before every N value.
  4. Sparse-read retention: program once and read only at selected times, avoiding repeated reads during the waiting interval.
  5. Read-disturb map: compare lower |VD,read| and different VG,read values while monitoring state separation and drift.
  6. Recovery experiment: program, wait at zero bias, and test whether the current relaxes logarithmically, as expected for detrapping.
  7. Independent ferroelectric verification: compare against PUND, capacitance, or switching-current measurements where possible.

9. Final updated conclusion

The device exhibits FeFET-like, reversible, write-polarity-dependent conductance switching. The best overall FeFET-like response was observed at VG,read = +0.5 V and VD,read = −2 V, where multiple pulses progressively established two conductance states and the training direction reversed reproducibly with write polarity over multiple cycles. The condition VG,read = −0.5 V and VD,read = +2 V remains important as the largest single-pulse retention window, but it is not the strongest demonstration of full FeFET switching. The difference indicates that one short pulse may switch too small a fraction of domains to reveal the gradual response. A matched 5 s read-only control shows only a small unidirectional drift and noise, so reading alone cannot explain the full switching amplitude. Nevertheless, charge trapping, detrapping, read disturbance, and possible high-field degradation materially influence the measured response. The most defensible current conclusion is a mixed polarization-and-trapping mechanism that requires fresh-device controls, lower-stress reads, interleaved polarity, and preset-controlled pulse-number measurements for quantitative separation.
